# Lecture notes: heteroskedasticity in linear regression

**Setting.** In the linear model \(y = X\beta + u\), ordinary least squares (OLS) gives
\(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\). Unbiasedness needs only exogeneity, \(E[u \mid X] = 0\). The usual formula for the
variance of \(\hat{\beta}\), \(\sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}\), needs one more assumption: homoskedasticity, a constant error
variance, \(Var(u_i \mid X) = \sigma^2\) for every observation.

**What heteroskedasticity is.** The error variance changes with the regressors. Typical case: in a regression of
household spending on income, spending varies much more among high-income households than among low-income ones.

**What it breaks, and what it does not.**
- OLS stays unbiased and consistent (exogeneity still holds).
- The usual standard errors are wrong, so t-tests and confidence intervals are invalid; in the spending example
  they are usually too small, so we reject the null too often.
- OLS is no longer efficient: the Gauss-Markov theorem needs homoskedasticity, so OLS is not BLUE (best linear
  unbiased estimator) any more.

**How to detect it.**
- Plot the residuals against the fitted values: a funnel shape is the classic sign.
- Breusch-Pagan test: regress the squared residuals on the regressors; under the null of homoskedasticity,
  \(nR^2\) from that auxiliary regression follows a chi-squared distribution with k degrees of freedom.
- White test: same idea, adding squares and cross-products of the regressors, so it also catches non-linear
  forms, at the cost of many degrees of freedom.

**What to do.**
1. Keep OLS and use heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (White, 1980). The "sandwich" estimator replaces
   \(\sigma^2 X'X\) in the middle by \(\sum_i \hat{u}_i^2 x_i x_i'\). Valid in large samples whatever the form of
   heteroskedasticity. This is the default in applied work.
2. Weighted least squares (WLS): if the form of the variance is known, e.g. \(Var(u_i \mid x_i) = \sigma^2 h(x_i)\),
   divide each observation by \(\sqrt{h(x_i)}\); the transformed model is homoskedastic and WLS is efficient.
   If \(h\) is estimated (feasible GLS), efficiency holds only asymptotically and a wrong \(h\) can hurt.
3. Transform the variable: logs often reduce heteroskedasticity in money variables.

**Questions raised in class.**
- Q: If the coefficients are still unbiased, why care? A: because inference is what we report; a wrong standard
  error turns a noisy estimate into a "significant" one.
- Q: Should robust standard errors always be used? A: in large samples they cost little; in small samples they can
  be biased downward, so small-sample corrections (HC2, HC3) are preferred.
- Open: when exactly does WLS with an estimated variance function beat OLS with robust errors in practice?

# Case notes: unit economics of a subscription box (fictitious company)

**The company.** Northwind Coffee Club sells a monthly coffee subscription at $30. Cost of goods and shipping per
box is $18, so the contribution margin is $12 per subscriber per month (40%). Customers are acquired mostly
through paid social ads.

**The numbers the team tracks.**
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC): marketing spend divided by new subscribers in the same period. Last quarter:
  $90,000 spent, 1,500 new subscribers → CAC of $60.
- Monthly churn: share of subscribers who cancel each month. Currently 8%.
- Expected lifetime in months ≈ 1 / churn → about 12.5 months at 8%.
- Lifetime value (LTV), on margin, not revenue: $12 × 12.5 ≈ $150.
- LTV / CAC ≈ 2.5. A common rule of thumb asks for at least 3 before scaling paid acquisition.
- CAC payback: months of margin needed to recover CAC = $60 / $12 = 5 months.

**The decision on the table.** Marketing wants to double ad spend. Two problems: (1) CAC usually rises as spend
grows, because the cheapest audiences are reached first; (2) at 8% churn, LTV/CAC is already below the target.

**Levers discussed.**
- Reduce churn: an annual plan paid upfront with a discount; a "skip a month" button instead of cancel. Cutting
  churn from 8% to 6% raises expected lifetime to about 16.7 months and LTV to about $200.
- Raise margin: a price increase to $33 for new subscribers, tested on a share of traffic first.
- Measure CAC by channel and cohort, not blended: a blended CAC hides an expensive channel behind a cheap one.

**Pitfalls the mentor pointed out.**
- LTV computed on revenue instead of margin overstates value by 2.5× here.
- Churn in the first month is usually much higher than later; one average churn rate hides it, so look at cohort
  retention curves.
- Payback matters for cash: a 5-month payback means each new subscriber is a cash outflow for 5 months, so fast
  growth burns cash even when LTV/CAC looks fine.

**Open question.** Is the annual plan's discount worth the lower margin, given that it also brings the cash
forward?

style Ddeep-studylens: technical-quantitative

What heteroskedasticity breaks and what it does not · theory

  • OLS stays unbiased and consistent: Unbiasedness needs only exogeneity E[u|X]=0, which still holds when error variance varies with the regressors, so the estimates themselves are fine.
  • Standard errors are wrong: The usual sigma^2(X'X)^-1 formula assumes homoskedasticity. With heteroskedasticity, t-tests and confidence intervals are invalid; in the spending example they tend to be too small, so the null is rejected too often.
  • OLS loses efficiency: Gauss-Markov requires homoskedasticity, so under heteroskedasticity OLS is not BLUE any more.

Detecting heteroskedasticity · theory

  • Residuals against fitted values: A funnel shape in the plot is the classic visual sign.
  • Breusch-Pagan test: Regress squared residuals on the regressors; under the null of homoskedasticity, n times R^2 from that auxiliary regression is chi-squared with k degrees of freedom. APPLIED
  • White test: Same logic as Breusch-Pagan but the auxiliary regression also includes squares and cross-products of the regressors, so it catches non-linear forms at the cost of many degrees of freedom.

Remedies · theory

  • Robust standard errors (sandwich estimator): Keep OLS and replace sigma^2 X'X in the middle with the sum of squared residuals times x_i x_i'. Valid in large samples whatever the form of heteroskedasticity; the default in applied work. VERIFY
  • Weighted least squares with known variance form: If Var(u_i|x_i)=sigma^2 h(x_i), divide each observation by sqrt(h(x_i)). The transformed model is homoskedastic and WLS is efficient.
  • Feasible GLS with estimated h: Efficiency holds only asymptotically, and a wrong specification of h can hurt.
  • Log transformation: Taking logs of money variables often reduces heteroskedasticity.

Small samples and robust errors · theory

  • Small-sample corrections HC2 and HC3: In small samples robust standard errors can be biased downward, so HC2 or HC3 corrections are preferred. Cheap in large samples, so robust errors are a safe default there. VERIFY

Doubts raised

  • If the coefficients are still unbiased, why care? → Because inference is what gets reported; a wrong standard error can turn a noisy estimate into a significant one.
  • Should robust standard errors always be used? → In large samples they cost little; in small samples they can be biased downward, so HC2 or HC3 are preferred.

Still open

  • When exactly does WLS with an estimated variance function beat OLS with robust errors in practice?
style Bdeep-studylens: decision/business

Unit economics core · theory

  • Contribution margin: Price $30 minus COGS and shipping $18 gives $12 per subscriber per month, a 40% margin. Value is measured on this margin, not on revenue.
  • CAC: Customer acquisition cost is marketing spend divided by new subscribers in the same period. Example: $90,000 over 1,500 new subscribers gives $60.
  • LTV on margin: Lifetime value is margin per month times expected lifetime: $12 × 12.5 ≈ $150.
  • LTV/CAC target: LTV/CAC is about 2.5 here, below the rule-of-thumb minimum of 3 before scaling paid acquisition. VERIFY
  • CAC payback: Payback is CAC divided by monthly margin: $60 / $12 = 5 months. Each new subscriber is a cash outflow for those 5 months.

Churn and lifetime · theory

  • Expected lifetime: Expected lifetime in months ≈ 1 / monthly churn. At 8% churn that is about 12.5 months. REVERSE
  • Churn sensitivity: Cutting churn from 8% to 6% raises lifetime to about 16.7 months and LTV to about $200.
  • First-month churn: Early churn is usually much higher than later churn. A single average rate hides it, so read cohort retention curves.

Pitfalls in the reasoning · theory

  • Revenue instead of margin: Computing LTV on revenue overstates value by 2.5× here (30 / 12). APPLIED
  • Fast growth burns cash: With a 5-month payback, LTV/CAC can look healthy while growth still drains cash. Check payback next to LTV/CAC.

Measuring and testing levers · tools

  • CAC by channel and cohort: Measure CAC per channel and per cohort, not blended. A blended figure hides an expensive channel behind a cheap one. APPLIED
  • Cohort retention curves: Build retention by signup cohort to see first-month drop-off separately from later churn.
  • Price test on a traffic share: Test a price rise to $33 for new subscribers on part of the traffic before rolling it out. This is a margin lever.

Northwind decision: double ad spend · story

  • The decision: Marketing wants to double paid ads. CAC tends to rise with spend because the cheapest audiences are reached first, and LTV/CAC is already below 3 at 8% churn.
  • Levers on the table: Churn levers: annual prepaid plan with discount, skip-a-month button. Margin lever: price rise to $33 for new subscribers. Measurement: CAC by channel and cohort.

Still open

  • Is the annual plan's discount worth the lower margin, given that it also brings the cash forward?
